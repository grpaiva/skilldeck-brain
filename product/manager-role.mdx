## The position we take

Skill Deck does the analysis. The manager does the leading.

We are explicit, in the pitch and in the product, that the human role of the leader is essential to development. The tool is not trying to replace the manager with an algorithm — it is trying to remove the two things that stop managers from developing their people: no time, and no specifics.

The playbook states the division cleanly: the manager's job is **not to replace the product's weekly autopilot**. It is to coach, guide, and hold accountable.

## What a manager sees

Two surfaces.

### Team page

Cycle stats and the member Tracks timeline for the team. This is where stuck states show up: an empty member row, a Pending status, a member lagging peers on the timeline.

### Member page

One person in depth. The playbook's standard prep order is **Overview → Journal → Deck**, and those are the three things a manager reads:

| Surface | What the manager reads |
| --- | --- |
| **Overview** | Progress vs Goal, days or weeks on the current Track, Track history — completed, still active, switched, rolled, expired — and the Cycle's evaluation count |
| **Journal** | The member's Kickoff, Weekly Prep, Weekly Review, and Wrap-Up dispatches |
| **Deck** | Current scores and confidence across the member's Skills, plus a callout that a quarterly report exists |
| **Track analyses** | The per-meeting summary for the tracked Skill, used for the one-meeting deep-dive |
| **Track Settings** | Per-member overrides and the whitelist |

<Warning>
  The manager's view stops there, by design and in code. They cannot open an individual evidence page, cannot click through from a Track analysis to the meeting behind it, cannot read the member's **Evidence Evaluated** dispatches, and cannot open the body of their report. A member sees more about themselves than their manager does.

  See [Privacy and data](/product/privacy-and-data) before answering any question about this.
</Warning>

## What a manager can do

<AccordionGroup>
  <Accordion title="Set up the team" icon="settings">
    On the team page: enable Skill Tracks, and set automation and the whitelist. This is step two of the playbook's six moves, and skipping it is the most common setup failure.
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="Whitelist Skills" icon="list-filter">
    Mark the Skills most relevant for a person or a team — matched to job scope, personal challenges, or organizational values. It keeps Track focus role-aligned without the manager choosing every Track by hand.

    It resolves **most specific wins**: a member-level whitelist overrides a team one, which overrides an organization one. With no whitelist anywhere, every Skill is allowed.

    <Warning>
      The whitelist controls **which Skills a member can start a Track on**. It is not a visibility filter — a manager still sees every scored Skill on the member's Deck, whitelisted or not.
    </Warning>

    Its only other effect is a mild bias in evidence triage, and only when the member has **no active Track**. Once a Track is active, triage runs unbiased, so the tracked Skill's score keeps reflecting what the evidence shows. Evidence has to turn up on its own to count.

    That last detail is the answer to "is it just going to tell me what I want to hear?"
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="Start, configure, and edit Tracks" icon="flag">
    A manager can start a Track, configure a pending Skill or Goal, and edit an active Track.

    They can also leave it to automation. The playbook recommends auto-assigning the Skill and Goal at the Monthly and Quarterly levels, so a member is never blocked waiting on a manager decision.
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="Set optional Targets" icon="target">
    Targets can be created for an individual member or a whole team. Two kinds: a **Skill target** on specific scores, or a **confidence target** to reach a minimum average Deck confidence.

    Targets are optional in the playbook's agendas. The confidence target is the right first one for a new team, because nothing can be developed until the profiles mean something.
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="Review at the end of a period" icon="calendar-check">
    At the end of each Track, month, or Cycle, a manager has data to review both how the tool is being used and how the person is developing. The Cycle-end pipeline generates snapshots and reports automatically, which is what makes quarter-over-quarter comparison possible.
  </Accordion>
</AccordionGroup>

## The coaching cadence

A manager picks a **management level** — Weekly, Monthly, or Quarterly — based on team size, seniority, Skill criticality, and how mature the team's evidence is. Levels are named for how often the _manager_ meets, not for how often Skill Deck emails the member; the product sends Monday Prep and Friday Review every week regardless.

## One-to-ones

The coaching meeting _is_ the one-to-one, and it is where Skill Deck pays off: the manager arrives with progress against Goal, themes from the week's Prep and Review, and a specific meeting to walk through.

<Warning>
  There is **no dedicated 1-on-1 feature**. Sessions, agendas, and notes are not product objects. The playbook supplies the agenda; the product supplies the material.

  Describe the one-to-one as the use case the manager surfaces support, not as a module.
</Warning>

## The failure mode to design against

Across all three management levels, the playbook names the same one: **Skill churn**. Switching Skills so often that Tracks never reach the two-week completion window.

It is worth understanding why this is the recurring risk. A manager who is engaged wants to act, and the most available action is changing the Skill. The playbook's default bias — keep the Track unless there is a strong reason to switch — exists to redirect that energy into coaching practice quality instead.

## The limits

A manager's access is bounded by the product's privacy model, and those bounds are a selling point rather than a limitation.

- **No transcripts.** After 7 days no transcript exists anywhere in the system, and raw content is never written to the database.
- **Only the member's own behavior is evaluated.** A meeting produces evidence about the Skill Deck user it came from, not about their colleagues.

## Who counts as a manager

Access to a member's page requires one of:

- **Team Manager** on that member's team, or on any **ancestor team** — manager access cascades down to sub-teams.
- **Organization Owner or Admin**, who get manager-equivalent access across the organization.

There is no separate "direct report" object. Management is expressed through team membership plus the Manager role.

## What the member is told

The member learns the shape of manager access from the **User Guide**, opened from the sidebar and published at `/knowledge-base`. It names both halves. A manager can open the Deck and its confidence, Cycle progress, Tracks with their history and settings, the four Journal dispatches, and the Track analyses. Moments, Evidence Evaluated posts, and raw evidence content stay with the member. The disclosure ships in English, Portuguese, and Spanish.

A member reads a superset of that on their own Overview, Deck, and Journal, so a manager holds nothing the member has not already seen.

Every dispatch is addressed to the member alone. A manager reaches four of the five by opening the member's Journal on the member page.

Access follows the roles above and applies the same way to everyone on a team. The boundary lives in the authorization layer rather than in a per-member setting, so "who can see my data" has one answer for the whole team.
