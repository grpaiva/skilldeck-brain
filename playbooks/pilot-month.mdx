## When to use this

A team matching the [ICP](/company/icp-and-market) has agreed to try Skill Deck for a free month.

## Prerequisites

- The team fits the ICP: 10 to 249 employees, hybrid or remote.
- A named manager is committed as the internal owner. Without one, the pilot has no one to interpret the results, and it will not convert.
- Enough of the team runs recurring meetings that evidence will actually accumulate.

## Why a month

Skill Deck has nothing to show until evidence exists. A Track runs about two weeks minimum, and confidence in a Skill score climbs as evaluations accumulate. A one-week trial shows a prospect an empty product and teaches them the wrong thing about it.

The month is the shortest window where a team sees its own profiles fill in — which is the moment the product starts selling itself.

## Steps

<Steps>
  <Step title="Set the expectation before day one">
    Tell the owner directly that the first week will look thin and the profiles get interesting in weeks two and three. A prospect who is surprised by an empty dashboard on day three is a prospect who has already churned.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Get calendars connected on day one">
    This is the only thing the user has to do, and the entire pilot depends on it. Connected calendars on day one, for everyone in the pilot, is the single strongest predictor of whether the month produces anything.

    If the team meets on Zoom, they also need the Zoom connection — a calendar alone will not get a bot into a Zoom call. Leave Slack out of the first weeks.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Brief the team on privacy up front">
    Say what a manager can and cannot see before anyone asks. Use [Privacy and data](/product/privacy-and-data). Unaddressed privacy anxiety is the quietest way a pilot dies — people stop taking meetings with the bot and nobody says why.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Give the manager the coaching playbook">
    Hand over the [Manager Coaching Playbook](/playbooks/manager-coaching/overview) and have them pick a level. For a pilot, Weekly is usually right: the playbook recommends it explicitly for teams new to Skill Deck and for teams still unlocking Skills.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Let it run">
    Users work normally. Dispatches arrive by email. Resist adding process beyond the manager's chosen cadence; the low-friction claim is the product's, and a pilot loaded with check-ins does not test it.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Check in mid-pilot">
    Around the two-week mark, confirm calendars are still connected and evidence is landing. Fix integration problems now, while there is still time for the second half of the month to produce something.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Run the wrap-up with the manager">
    At the end of the month, walk the manager through their team view and one individual in depth. The conversion argument is their own team's data, not a feature list.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Convert">
    Move to the plan that matches their seat count. See [Business model](/company/business-model). Do not discount without a founder.
  </Step>
</Steps>

## Definition of done

- Everyone in the pilot connected a calendar in week one.
- Each participant has enough evidence for a meaningful profile by the end of the month.
- The manager has seen their team view and at least one individual in detail.
- A paid plan is agreed, or you know precisely why it was not.

## Failure modes

**Calendars never get connected.** The most common way a pilot produces nothing. Treat connection rate as the pilot's health metric from day one, not as a setup detail.

**No internal owner.** A pilot with no manager attached generates profiles nobody looks at. Individual contributors will not sell it upward on their own.

**Judging it in week one.** Confidence is low early by design. If someone evaluates the product in the first few days, they are evaluating the absence of evidence.

**Skipping the privacy conversation.** It does not go away. It goes underground.
