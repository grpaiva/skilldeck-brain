## Ideal customer profile

<Columns cols={2}>
  <Card title="Company size" icon="building-2">
    SMBs with **10 to 249 employees**.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Work model" icon="laptop">
    **Hybrid or remote** teams.
  </Card>
</Columns>

The work model is not a side detail. Skill Deck reads behavior from meetings, and hybrid and remote teams run their real work through scheduled calls. A fully co-located team makes most of its decisions in rooms we never see, which weakens the evidence base.

The size band is a deliberate compromise. Below roughly 10 people there is no manager layer for the product to serve. Above roughly 250 the buyer becomes an HR function with an incumbent HRIS, a procurement process, and a longer cycle — a market we intend to reach later, not first.

## Who feels the pain

| Role | What they feel | What Skill Deck gives them |
| --- | --- | --- |
| HR / People lead | Development is their mandate but they cannot make managers act on it | A tool to hand to leadership that puts development on the agenda |
| Manager | Wants to develop their team, has no time and no specifics to work from | A per-person view with evidence-backed levels, plus a coaching cadence to run |
| Individual contributor | Gets one round of vague feedback a year | A granular profile and a Track with actions they can take this week |

HR is usually the buyer. The manager is the user who determines whether it sticks. The individual contributor is the one who has to feel it was worth it.

## Target markets

The first wave targets the **United States, Mexico, and Brazil**.

The product speaks the user's language in its communications, so market entry is not gated on a rewrite. Founder-market fit in Brazil is 12 years deep, which makes it the fastest market to test in; the US is the market that determines whether the business is large.

## Go-to-market motion: land grab

We are running the land-grab play as Y Combinator describes it: sign up as many customers as possible in the shortest possible time, across different industries and markets, rather than perfecting one segment first.

<Steps>
  <Step title="Give it away for a month">
    An aggressive sales motion offers the tool free to our ICP. A team uses it for one month and measures results on their own people.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Convert to paid">
    After the trial month, the team moves to a paid plan. See [Business model](/company/business-model) for the plan structure.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Spread horizontally">
    Push across industries and across the US, Mexico, and Brazil at the same time, rather than saturating one vertical before moving on.
  </Step>
</Steps>

The free month is doing real work in this motion. Skill Deck needs evidence before it can say anything interesting, so a trial that is too short shows a prospect an empty product. A month is roughly the shortest window in which a team sees its own profiles fill in — which is also the moment the product sells itself.

## Competitive framing

Do not position Skill Deck against meeting recorders or against performance review software. It loses both comparisons on features and wins neither on price.

Position it on the seam between them:

- **Meeting intelligence tools** capture what was said. They stop at the transcript and the summary. They have no model of the person and no development cycle.
- **Performance and L&D platforms** have Skill frameworks and development plans, but every input is self-reported or manager-reported. They inherit exactly the perception problem we exist to remove.

Skill Deck is the only one doing both halves: observed behavior in real meetings feeding a development cycle that runs continuously.
